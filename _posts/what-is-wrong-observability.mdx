In one of my previous roles we ran GlusterFS on OpenShift. Most problems yielded to our
runbooks and scripts. When they did not, we called the person who had set it up. Our F5
load balancers were the same story: two or three people knew them well, and things went
faster when they were in the room.

That is not a bad setup. It is how open source works too.

<PullQuote cite="Linus's law">
  Given enough eye balls, all bugs are shallow.
</PullQuote>

Even Rust and Kubernetes have a handful of people responsible for each component, and
curl, one of the most used programs in the world, is maintained by one person.

But I digress. Let's start with observability. I want to start with the definition,
so we are thinking of the same thing.
I define observability as the ability to be informed ~~if~~ **when** things break as soon as possible,
have enough information to identify the root cause or a reasonable hypothesis of the root cause.
We want the information to be available for everyone in the team.

## Chapter 1: Just click and drag!

![Grafana k8s dashboard](https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/eb110407-ab7b-4025-8ceb-a79835c60346)

We have all seen dashboards like the one above. They are easy to make: write a query, drag
a panel into place. A new data source is a few clicks. So are users, Slack and PagerDuty.
That ease of use is exactly what is wrong with them. Every editable thing in that UI is a
problem the tool now has to solve, and none of those problems are about your system.

<NumberedList long>

- **State** Because the dashboards are editable, the tool needs a database. Now someone
  owns backups, encryption and what happens when two people edit the same panel. None of
  this is about observing your system. It exists only because the UI is where the dashboard
  lives.

- **Shadow permissions** Because the dashboards are editable, the tool needs its own answer to
  who may change what, plus an audit trail of who changed it. Your company already has that answer:
  Kubernetes RBAC and cloud IAM. Now there is a second copy, kept in the observability tool, that
  drifts from the first one as people join, leave and change teams.

- **Forest for trees** Because the dashboards are editable, there is no way to state what
  we want and let the tool work it out. What we want is one line: are there deadlocks in
  Postgres? Is anything returning errors it did not return yesterday? Instead we describe
  how to draw it: which panel, which row, which data source, who may see it. The intent is
  a sentence, and it is buried under a hundred settings about placement.

- **Not in the repo** - Because the UI are editable, none of this goes through the
  workflow we already trust for everything else. Code lands in a git repo, someone reviews
  the pull request, and a pipeline builds and deploys it. Adding a user, a data source or an
  alert rule to the dashboard is a click in a UI. There is no review, no history to bisect,
  no rollback, and no way to bring the same setup up in the next cluster except by clicking
  it together again from memory.

</NumberedList>

## Chapter 2: Maya tagged you on TikTok?

We have a tool that watches the system and is the first thing we reach for when something
breaks. Getting told is the solved part: PagerDuty wakes the on-call, and Slack tells the
rest of the team. What arrives is one line.

<Notification app="PagerDuty">Error rate above threshold</Notification>

Is it the database,
the last deploy, one customer with a bad script, or the usual Monday spike? The answer is
on the dashboard, and the dashboard is on a desk. So the person holding the phone has a
headline, and the person at a desk has the story. Everyone else waits for one of them.

Toyota solved this on a factory floor decades ago. Any worker on the line can pull the
andon cord when they see a defect. The cord is not the clever part. Every factory has a
way to stop the line. The board is the clever part. It lights up above the line and shows
the whole floor which station and what kind of defect, so the nearest person walks over
already knowing what they will find. The line stops only if they cannot fix it within a
minute or two, and most pulls never get that far. The problem is not routed to a
specialist. It is shown to everyone, with enough detail that the nearest pair of hands
can deal with it.

Knight Capital had the cord and not the board. On the morning of 1 August 2012 their
trading system sent 97 emails warning that a routing flag was misconfigured. The emails
went to a handful of people, and none of them read the messages as an alert, because
nothing in them said what would happen next or what to do. Forty-five minutes after the
market opened the firm had lost 440 million dollars, and by the end of the year it no
longer existed. The warning was there. What was missing was the picture behind it, in
front of someone who could act.

That is the difference between minutes and hours. Not everyone needs to start solving the
problem. But everyone who could solve it needs to see it, wherever they are, on whatever
they have in their hand. Look at your own phone. Messages from family, friends and doctors
arrive there. Most can wait until evening. Some need a follow-up question right now, and
one or two mean you drop everything and drive to the hospital. You can make that call
because the message reached you in full, not as a one-line summary relayed by someone at
a desk.

Our observability tools deliver the summary to the phone and keep the picture at the desk.
The graph that says which service is on fire lives in a browser tab, behind a login, in a
layout someone else designed for a 27-inch screen. The board never went home with anyone.

## Chapter 3: [One universal standard number 14](https://xkcd.com/927/)

I have been working on a [new tool](https://vedavid.dev) based on the ideas above.
It is declarative, and it is designed for phones and lets you view Prometheus metrics on the go.

![How vedavid fits together](/assets/blog/what-is-wrong-observability/architecture.svg)

It has three parts. The [connector](https://github.com/vedavid-dev/connector) is a small open-source program that runs inside your
Kubernetes cluster. It reads your dashboard definitions, fetches the matching
metrics from Prometheus, and sends the results out. It needs no special cluster
permissions and never accepts incoming connections; it only reaches out. The relay
is a service hosted by Vedavid that sits in the middle. It securely connects each phone
to the right cluster and makes sure users only ever see their own organization's data.
The iOS app is where engineers view dashboards. It's deliberately read-only:
you look at dashboards on the phone, but you never build or edit them there.

[Dashboards](https://github.com/vedavid-dev/dashboards) are written in a simple,
[declarative YAML DSL](https://github.com/vedavid-dev/dashboard-dsl) (a small, purpose-built
language for describing dashboards) and kept in git alongside the rest of your infrastructure,
so they're reviewed and versioned like code. The files declare what to show, not where
to put it, and the phone decides how to lay everything out for a small screen.

[Vedavid](https://vedavid.dev) focuses only on metrics. It intentionally leaves out logs, traces,
and alerting so it can do one job well: giving on-call engineers a fast,
clear view of what's happening when they get paged.

I'd love for you to try [Vedavid](https://vedavid.dev).
The iOS app is available on [TestFlight](https://testflight.apple.com/join/CTSQrsnb).
Install it, sign in with your Gmail address, and you'll land in a live demo environment
with real dashboards. There's no cluster to set up and no connector to install.
